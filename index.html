Part 1 · PICO Explained Simply9 / 48
A recipe for your research question

What is PICO?

PICO is just a simple checklist with 4 ingredients — like a recipe. If your question has all 4, it will work beautifully in Elicit.

P
People
Who are you studying?
"adults with type 2 diabetes"
I
Intervention
What treatment or approach?
"metformin medication"
C
Comparison
Compared to what?
"placebo or diet only"
O
Outcome
What are you measuring?
"HbA1c blood sugar levels"
🍳 Combined: "In adults with type 2 diabetes (P), does metformin (I) compared to placebo (C) reduce HbA1c levels (O)?" — this is the exact question in our live demo!

Part 1 · Not doing clinical research?11 / 48
For qualitative & social research

Try PEO Instead of PICO

PEO is even simpler — just 3 parts. Use it when you're exploring an experience rather than comparing two treatments.

P
People — Who? "PhD students"
E
Experience — What happened? "COVID-19 lockdown"
O
Outcome — What exploring? "mental health and wellbeing"
"How did COVID-19 lockdown (E) affect the mental health (O) of PhD students (P) in Kenya?"
Which to use?
Use PICO when:
  • You're comparing two treatments, methods, or approaches
  • You have a clear control/comparison group
  • Health, clinical, or quantitative research
Use PEO when:
  • You're exploring someone's experience or perspective
  • Qualitative, social, or education research
  • No clear comparison group

Part 2 · Screening papers18 / 48
Deciding which papers qualify

Inclusion & Exclusion Criteria

You can't use every paper you find. You need rules for which papers qualify for your review.

✅ Include a paper if...
  • It studies the right population
  • Published in the right time period
  • Measures the outcome you care about
  • It's a peer-reviewed journal article
❌ Exclude a paper if...
  • Conference abstract only (not full paper)
  • Opinion/editorial, not a study
  • Animal or lab studies (not human)
  • Too old or wrong language

Part 3 · Writing column questions25 / 48
This matters a lot

Good vs Bad Column Questions

❌ Vague (bad)

"Results?"

Too vague. What results exactly?

"Is this a good paper?"

Subjective. AI can't judge quality.

"Results, methods, sample, limitations?"

4 things in 1. Split into 4 columns.

✅ Specific (good)

"What was the main finding?"

Clear. One fact at a time.

"How many participants were in this study?"

One specific, findable fact.

"In which country was this study conducted?"

Objective. One answer.

✅ Simple rule: One column = one fact. Ask about one specific thing. End with "?"

Part 3 · Writing from your table29 / 48
The most important writing skill

Write a Synthesis Paragraph

❌ Don't write descriptions:
"Hirst (2012) found X. Garber (1997) found Y."
✅ Write synthesis:
"Evidence consistently shows that metformin reduces HbA1c compared to placebo, with effects ranging from 0.6–2.0% across multiple study designs (Hirst et al., 2012; Garber et al., 1997). Most studies were from high-income countries, suggesting a gap in evidence from sub-Saharan Africa."
3-sentence formula
SENTENCE 1 — main pattern
"Most studies found that [X] leads to [Y]..."
SENTENCE 2 — variation / nuance
"However, the effect varied depending on [Z]..."
SENTENCE 3 — gap you noticed
"No studies from [context] were found, suggesting..."
💡 Your Elicit table gives you all the evidence for these 3 sentences. Just look at the patterns in your columns.

Part 4 · Prompt Engineering31 / 48
In plain English

What is "Prompt Engineering"?

A "prompt" is simply what you type into an AI tool. "Prompt engineering" means learning how to type things better so the AI gives you better answers.

🔧 Analogy: A Google search for "Kenya" gives different results from "best hospitals in Nairobi Kenya 2024". Same engine — different quality of prompt = very different results.
🎯 In Elicit specifically: Your search query AND your column questions are both prompts. Better prompts = better papers found + more accurate extractions.
The 5 prompt ingredients
1
Context — Who/what/where is this about?
2
Specificity — Exact terms, populations, timeframes
3
Format — Full sentences, not keywords
4
Outcome focus — What do you want to know?
5
Iteration — Try variations, compare results

Part 4 · Prompt Iteration33 / 48
Don't just search once

The Iteration Strategy

One of the most powerful prompt engineering skills: run multiple versions of the same search and compare results. Different words find different papers.

VERSION 1 — your PICO question
"Does metformin reduce HbA1c in adults with type 2 diabetes vs placebo over 12 months?"
→ Finds: clinical trials, meta-analyses
VERSION 2 — broaden the terms
"How effective is biguanide therapy for glycaemic control in type 2 diabetic patients?"
→ Finds: older papers using different terminology
VERSION 3 — add context
"Does metformin reduce HbA1c in sub-Saharan African adults with type 2 diabetes?"
→ Finds: Africa-specific evidence (or reveals the gap!)
💡 Run all 3 versions. Export each CSV. Merge in Excel. Remove duplicates. Now you have a comprehensive, well-evidenced review.

Part 4 · Advanced prompt techniques35 / 48
Techniques that experienced researchers use

5 Advanced Elicit Prompting Techniques

🎯
1. Specify the evidence type

Add "in randomised controlled trials" or "in qualitative studies" to your query to filter by study design right in the prompt — before using Elicit's filters.

"...in RCTs published between 2015 and 2024"
🌍
2. Anchor to a geography

Adding a location narrows results and often reveals evidence gaps for your region.

"...in low-income countries in sub-Saharan Africa"
📐
3. Ask for magnitude

Don't just ask "does X work" — ask "how much" to get quantitative results.

"By how much does metformin reduce HbA1c levels?"
🔄
4. Use synonyms intentionally

Run a second search using alternative terms for your intervention to catch papers using different vocabulary.

metformin → biguanide → glucophage
💬
5. Use Chat with Papers for follow-up prompts

After your main search, use the chat box to dig deeper: "What were the most common limitations reported by authors?" or "Which paper had the longest follow-up period?"

Part 4 · Common prompt mistakes37 / 48
Learn from these errors

6 Common Prompt Mistakes & How to Fix Them

❌ Too short / keyword-style

"diabetes Kenya"

✅ Fix: "What interventions improve diabetes outcomes in Kenya?"
❌ No outcome specified

"studies about metformin in Africa"

✅ Fix: Add what you're measuring: "...reduce HbA1c in adults?"
❌ Too narrow too early

"metformin 500mg T2DM HbA1c RCT Kenya 2020"

✅ Fix: Start broad, then narrow with filters after seeing results.
❌ Column question too vague

Column: "Results?"

✅ Fix: "What was the primary outcome and the reported effect size?"
❌ Never iterating

Searching once, getting 5 results, concluding nothing exists

✅ Fix: Run 3 variations with different synonyms. Compare results.
❌ Using Elicit like Google

"best treatment diabetes 2024"

✅ Fix: Write a proper research question — not a Google search.

Important — Don't skip this slide39 / 48
Before you put anything in your thesis

Always Verify + Always Cite ⚠️

🤔 AI can be wrong — It reads millions of papers but can occasionally misread a number. A wrong statistic in your thesis is serious.
How to verify:
  • Click any cell → read the source quote
  • Go to the original paper → confirm the number
  • If "Unclear" — open the full paper yourself
  • Check 20% of extractions independently
⚠️ Any statistic you put in your thesis must be verified against the original paper. Not just Elicit's extraction.
Mandatory — declare AI use
Write in your Methods section:

Wrap-up44 / 48
What to remember from today

6 Things to Remember

1️⃣
Type full sentences, not keywords
Elicit understands meaning — the more specific, the better
2️⃣
PICO = P (people) + I (intervention) + C (comparison) + O (outcome)
A recipe — make sure all 4 ingredients are in your question
3️⃣
Iterate your prompts
Run 3 variations with synonyms — different words find different papers
4️⃣
Use columns to extract data automatically
One column = one specific question. Turn on suggested columns first.
5️⃣
Always verify numbers before using in thesis
Click cells, read source quotes, confirm in original paper
6️⃣
Declare you used Elicit in your Methods section
Transparency is mandatory — not optional
